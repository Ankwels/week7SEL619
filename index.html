Smart Contracts – Code is Law

Smart contracts are self-executing agreements that run exactly as programmed.
“A smart contract is a contractual agreement embedded in a self-enforceable piece of code.”— Kushwaha et al. (2022)

The WHEN → THEN logic

Smart contracts follow deterministic rules:

WHEN [condition is met] THEN [action executes automatically]

The Vending Machine Analogy

WHEN correct payment inserted → THEN product dispensed
  • No human middleman needed
  • Rules enforced automatically
  • Transaction is transparent

Consensus – Agreement Without Authority

Blockchain’s biggest trick: getting strangers to agree on truth without trusting anyone.

Consensus mechanisms are protocols that ensure all participants in a decentralised network agree on the state of the blockchain.

Proof of Work (PoW)

Miners compete to solve cryptographic puzzles. The first to solve it adds the next block and earns a reward.

Used by: Bitcoin, Ethereum (pre-2022)

Trade-off: high security, massive energy use

Proof of Stake (PoS)

Validators lock up cryptocurrency as collateral. The network selects them to propose and validate blocks, often in proportion to stake.

Used by: Ethereum (post-2022), Cardano, Solana

Trade-off: energy-efficient, needs capital (32 ETH)

The Merge (September 2022)

Ethereum successfully transitioned from Proof of Work to Proof of Stake.

Why it matters

  • 99.95% reduction in energy consumption
  • Faster transaction finality
  • More scalable for future growth
  • Maintains network security

Trade-off debate

  • Critics: “PoS favours the wealthy”
  • Supporters: “PoW favours equipment owners”
  • Reality: both have centralisation concerns

Gas Economics

Nothing is free – even on decentralised networks.

Gas is the unit that measures the computational effort needed to execute operations on Ethereum. Every transaction and smart contract execution requires gas.

Why gas exists

  1. Compensates validators for the computational work they perform
  2. Prevents spam attacks by making abuse economically expensive
  3. Allocates limited resources during network congestion

How Gas Works

  • Gas price: measured in “gwei” (1 ETH = 1 billion gwei)
  • Gas limit: the maximum amount you’re willing to pay for a transaction
  • Dynamic pricing: based on supply and demand – a busy network means higher fees
  • Total cost: gas used × gas price = transaction fee (in ETH)

⚠️ Common misconception

Myth: “The Merge to Proof of Stake eliminated gas fees.”

Reality: The Merge cut energy use by 99.95%, but gas fees remain necessary to pay validators and prevent spam. Layer 2 solutions (like Optimism and Arbitrum) are what dramatically reduce fees.

The Ethereum Stack

Think of a layered cake: each layer does a different job, from the base blockchain up to the user interface.

🧱 1. Blockchain layer

The foundation. Nodes (Geth, Nethermind) keep data in sync; validators who stake ETH secure it.

The database and trust system.

⚙️ 2. Smart contract layer (EVM)

Contracts written in Solidity or Vyper, run by the EVM on every node.

The “app engine”.

🧩 3. Application layer (dApps)

Uniswap, OpenSea, Aave. They talk to contracts via web3.js, ethers.js or wagmi.

The user-facing logic.

🌐 4. Interface layer

Web or mobile front ends (HTML, React, Next.js) that connect to wallets like MetaMask so users can sign transactions.

The window to Ethereum.

☁️ 5. Supporting infrastructure

Node providers (Infura, Alchemy), indexing (The Graph), Layer 2s (Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon), storage (IPFS, Arweave).

The internet plumbing.
